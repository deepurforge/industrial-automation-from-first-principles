TT-101 TEMPERATURE TRANSMITTER · RTD (Pt100)

From heat to resistance to a number

A platinum RTD doesn't measure temperature directly — it measures its own electrical resistance, which happens to rise in a very predictable way as it gets hotter. Everything downstream depends on that relationship holding true.

Physics

Why platinum, and why "almost" linear

Platinum is used because its resistance-vs-temperature relationship is extremely stable and repeatable from one probe to the next — two Pt100s from different manufacturers will track each other within fractions of a degree. The relationship shown on the plot is the simplified linear approximation. Over the full industrial range, the more rigorous Callendar–Van Dusen equation adds a quadratic correction term above 0 °C, and a cubic term below it — which is why a quality temperature transmitter stores a polynomial, not a single slope, and why cheap modules that assume pure linearity drift off calibration at the extremes of their range.

The RTD is passive: it produces no signal on its own. A small excitation current has to be pushed through it, and the resulting voltage drop is measured. That excitation current itself dissipates a tiny amount of power in the element — self-heating error — which is why excitation is deliberately kept low, typically around 1 mA or less.

Field practice

2-wire vs. 3-wire vs. 4-wire

The lead wires themselves have resistance, and that resistance adds directly to the reading in a naive 2-wire hookup — over a long cable run this can look like several degrees of error. A 3-wire configuration lets the transmitter subtract one lead's resistance from the measurement; a 4-wire configuration (Kelvin sensing) separates the excitation and sensing paths entirely and cancels lead resistance completely — the standard for laboratory-grade accuracy.

  • Open circuit — reading jumps to full-scale high (fail-safe upscale)
  • Short circuit — reading drops toward the low end of range
  • Lead resistance drift — slow, silent offset error that calibration checks miss unless the whole loop is tested
  • Self-heating — reads slightly high in still air/gas, less so in flowing liquid

Design rationale

The "live zero" is deliberate, not arbitrary

A signal that ran 0–20 mA would make 0 mA ambiguous — is the process genuinely at zero, or is the wire cut, the power dead, or the transmitter failed? Starting at 4 mA reserves the bottom of the range as a health check: any current below roughly 3.6 mA (per the NAMUR NE43 convention) is unambiguously a fault, never a valid process reading.

The same two wires that carry this signal can also carry power to the transmitter — this is what makes a "2-wire loop-powered transmitter" possible. The control-room power supply pushes current around the loop; the field transmitter varies its own internal resistance to set how much current gets through, from 4 mA at 0% up to 20 mA at 100%. No separate power wiring is needed in the field.

Beyond analog

HART — a digital signal riding on top

Most modern 4–20 mA transmitters also support HART (Highway Addressable Remote Transducer): a small, high-frequency digital signal superimposed on the analog current, invisible to the PLC's analog input but readable by a handheld communicator or a HART-capable I/O card. This lets a technician read diagnostics, re-range the instrument, or check its configuration without disturbing the 4–20 mA process signal it's also sending.

  • < 3.6 mA — broken wire, dead power supply, or failed transmitter
  • > 21 mA — over-range or a transmitter fault reporting saturation
  • Stuck at exactly one value — often a frozen transmitter, not a frozen process
  • Noisy / jumpy current — check grounding and shielding before suspecting the sensor itself

Reading the curve

What τ actually means

The time constant τ is the time it takes the measured value to close 63.2% of the remaining gap to the true value. After one τ, 63% of the step is captured; after five time constants, the measurement is within about 0.7% of the final value — which is why "5τ" is the common rule of thumb for when a sensor has effectively "settled." Try dragging the τ slider to its slowest setting and triggering a step: the measured trace visibly can't keep up.

This isn't a flaw unique to bad instruments — every real sensor has some τ, because it takes a nonzero amount of time for heat to conduct through a thermowell, for pressure to propagate through an impulse line, or for a float to physically move. The engineering question is never "does my sensor lag," it's "is the lag small enough relative to how fast my process actually changes."

Why this matters for control

Lag feeds straight into loop tuning

A PID controller tuned against a fast, low-lag measurement will often overshoot and oscillate if that same measurement is suddenly slower — the controller keeps "pushing" because it hasn't yet seen the process catch up, then overcorrects once the delayed reading finally arrives. This is one of the most common root causes of an oscillating loop that used to be stable: a fouled thermowell, a plugged impulse line, or a failing sensor element quietly increasing τ long before it fails outright.

  • Fast τ (<1s) — bare thermocouple junction, small-diameter probes
  • Moderate τ (1–5s) — typical thermowell-mounted RTD in flowing liquid
  • Slow τ (>10s) — heavily lagged thermowells, still air, large thermal mass

The theorem

Why 2× and not just "faster is better"

The Nyquist–Shannon theorem states that a continuous signal can be perfectly reconstructed from its samples only if the sample rate is at least twice the highest frequency component present. Drag the slider just under 10 Hz and the reconstructed trace doesn't just get "chunky" — it locks onto a completely different, lower apparent frequency. That's not a rendering artifact; it's exactly what happens inside a real ADC. Once aliasing occurs, the original high-frequency information is permanently lost — no amount of downstream processing can recover it, because the samples themselves are indistinguishable from a genuinely slower signal.

Real fix: filter before you sample

The anti-aliasing filter

Because aliasing can't be corrected after the fact, industrial data acquisition hardware places a low-pass anti-aliasing filter in the analog path, before the ADC, to remove any frequency content above the Nyquist limit before it can be misrepresented. This is standard practice in vibration monitoring, where mechanical signals routinely carry energy at gear-mesh and bearing-defect frequencies well above what a casual sample rate would safely capture.

Separately, every ADC also introduces quantization error — each sample gets rounded to the nearest representable digital step, an unavoidable ±½ LSB (least significant bit) uncertainty baked into digitization itself, independent of sampling rate.

  • Vibration monitoring — a 150 Hz bearing fault sampled at 200 Hz can alias into a false low-frequency signature
  • Slow PLC scan rates — a fast-moving analog input polled too infrequently can misrepresent genuine process transients
  • No anti-aliasing filter fitted — the single most common root cause of "phantom" high-frequency noise appearing as slow drift

Where the noise comes from

Electrical noise isn't random luck

Most field noise has an identifiable physical cause: electromagnetic interference (EMI) coupling in from nearby variable-frequency drives or power cabling, poor or missing cable shielding, unshielded twisted pairs instead of shielded twisted pairs, or a ground loop — two ground references sitting at slightly different potentials, driving an unwanted current directly through the signal path. Fixing the physical cause (proper shielding, single-point grounding, physical separation from power cabling) is almost always a better first move than filtering the symptom.

Filtering is a trade, not a free win

Smoother isn't automatically better

Toggle the filter on and watch the trace smooth out — but also notice it reacts a beat slower to sudden changes. A moving-average filter is itself a small first-order lag (see LT-103): every sample of averaging you add to clean up noise also adds delay to how quickly the reported value can respond to a genuine, fast process change. Over-filtering a fast-moving signal can hide a real transient as effectively as noise can hide a real signal — the right amount of filtering depends entirely on how fast the underlying process legitimately moves.

  • Too little filtering — noisy trend graphs, chattering alarms, jittery control output
  • Too much filtering — real events smeared out, delayed alarm response, sluggish control
  • Better fix when available — shielded/twisted-pair cabling and correct grounding, before reaching for a software filter
FY-106 CHARACTERISTIC CURVE · HYSTERESIS

The same input, two different outputs

Drag the input slider up, then back down. The output traces a different path each direction — a hysteresis loop. The gap between the two paths is error you cannot calibrate away with a single lookup table.

■ rising   ■ falling
What causes it

Mechanical and magnetic memory

Hysteresis comes from physical effects that "remember" which direction the input was moving: friction and backlash in mechanical linkages, elastic lag in a diaphragm or bourdon tube that doesn't spring back at exactly the same rate it deformed, and magnetic domain lag in electromagnetic sensors. The gap you're tracing on the plot is real lost information — the instrument genuinely cannot tell, from the output alone, whether it arrived at that value from above or below.

Not the same as dead band

Two different characteristic errors

Hysteresis and dead band are often confused. Dead band is a range of input change that produces no output change at all — the needle simply doesn't move until the input crosses a threshold. Hysteresis, shown here, is different: the output does move, but along a different path depending on direction. Neither can be removed with a simple one-point calibration adjustment — a single calibration certificate captures accuracy at that moment, not the instrument's full rising/falling behavior across its range, which is why hysteresis is usually reported as its own spec on a datasheet.

  • Typical magnitude — often specified as a % of span (e.g. ±0.1%)
  • Worse with wear — mechanical linkages loosen over time, widening the loop
  • Practical impact — a control valve approaching setpoint from opposite directions can settle at two slightly different actual positions

Calibration vs. adjustment vs. verification

Three activities people conflate constantly

Calibration establishes the relationship between a reference and the instrument's indication — it produces a documented comparison, nothing more. Verification checks whether an instrument still meets its stated accuracy requirement. Adjustment is the separate, optional act of physically changing the instrument to bring it closer to the reference. An instrument can be calibrated and found out of tolerance without ever being adjusted — the calibration certificate is valid either way, because its job is to document the error, not eliminate it.

How uncertainty actually combines

Errors don't simply add up

Each link in the pyramid contributes its own uncertainty, evaluated either statistically from repeated readings (Type A) or from other evidence like a manufacturer datasheet or calibration certificate (Type B). These contributions combine as a root-sum-of-squares, not a simple sum, because independent error sources partially cancel: u = √(u₁² + u₂² + u₃² + …). This is why a five-link traceability chain isn't five times worse than a one-link chain — but it is still worse, and every additional link between a field instrument and the SI definition is one more place a documentation gap can quietly break the chain.

  • Expired calibration — the most common, and most preventable, traceability failure
  • Undocumented "field adjustment" — breaks the paper trail even if the instrument itself is fine
  • Wrong reference standard for the range — a reference calibrated for a different span than the DUT introduces silent extra error
