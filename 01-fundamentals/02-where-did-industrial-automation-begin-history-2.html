DeepURForge — 02 · History

Where did industrial automation begin?

Not with PLCs, robots, or computers. It evolved step by step — through mechanical machines, feedback, electrical control, electronics, and finally computing and networked intelligence. Scroll through 250 years of that evolution, animated.

Every technology on this timeline is a variation on one loop.
Measure → compare → correct → measure again. James Watt built it from brass in 1788. A modern PLC runs the same loop in software, ten thousand times a second.

02
1760s–1800s · First Industrial Revolution

Mechanization — muscle gets replaced first

Water and steam power replaced human and animal muscle. Industry 1.0. But control was still entirely manual — mechanization replaces effort, not judgment.

Animation — a steam engine in continuous motion
  • Steam power replaced muscle as the primary energy source.
  • Factories concentrated production around one shared power source.
Important distinction: mechanization replaced muscle. A human was still the controller — pulling levers, watching gauges. Automation, which replaces judgment, comes next.
03
1788 · The beginning of automatic control

Feedback: the single most important idea in this history

James Watt adapted the centrifugal governor to his steam engine in 1788. As speed rises, spinning flyballs fly outward under centrifugal force; that motion pulls a linkage that closes the steam valve, slowing the engine back down — with no person involved. Try it below.

The mechanism has exactly three moving parts: a pair of weighted arms hinged to a spinning shaft, a sliding collar the arms push against, and a linkage from that collar to the steam valve. Faster shaft → wider arm swing → collar rises → valve closes → less steam → shaft slows → arms fall back in. The engine never settles perfectly still; it "hunts" in a tight band around its target speed, correcting itself forever.

Push speed up — the valve closes automatically to slow the engine back down. That correction, with zero human input, is feedback.

The same loop lives inside every PID controller, PLC control block, and SCADA alarm limit covered later in this repository: measure → compare → correct → measure again.
04
1801 · Machines begin following instructions

The Jacquard loom — programmability is born

Chained punched cards told the loom which threads to raise for each pass. The loom followed a stored, repeatable sequence — the direct ancestor of every "program → controller → output" system since.

Animation — punched card feeding through the reader
  • Joseph Marie Jacquard demonstrated his loom in Lyon, France, in 1801.
  • Punched cards → mechanical selection → pattern, repeatably, without a weaver's constant judgment.
Program → controller → output. Punched cards later inspired Babbage's Analytical Engine — a direct line runs from this 1801 loom to the paper tape that programmed early PLCs 170 years later.

Electric motors let power be placed exactly where a process needed it, instead of one steam engine driving everything through belts and shafts. Industry 2.0.

06
1947 · From pneumatic/mechanical to electronic

The transistor changes everything downstream

Pneumatic and electromechanical control had physical limits. The transistor — small, reliable, solid-state — is the single component that made every digital controller after it physically possible.

A vacuum tube amplifies or switches current by heating a filament until it boils electrons off a cathode, inside a fragile glass envelope that needs seconds to warm up and constantly drains power as heat. A transistor does the same job by shifting current through a sliver of doped silicon or germanium — no filament, no glass, no warm-up, and a fraction of the power draw. That difference alone is why every control system after 1947 got smaller, cooler, and faster in the same breath.

A vacuum-tube computer like ENIAC needed roughly 18,000 tubes, filled a room, and a tube failed on average every couple of days. A single modern microcontroller now holds billions of transistor-equivalent switches on one chip smaller than a fingernail.

A general-purpose computer supervised a live industrial process for one of the first times — coordinating measurements that used to be split across dozens of separate instruments.

08
1968–1970 · Modicon 084

The PLC — logic becomes software

GM asked for an electronic replacement for relay panels. Bedford Associates, led by Dick Morley, delivered the Modicon 084 — the 84th project at the firm, and the first commercially deployed PLC.

Interactive — toggle between before and after
BEFORE — a wall of wired relay logic
  • Every relay-logic change meant physically rewiring the panel — expensive and slow.
  • The 084 was programmed in Ladder Logic, deliberately designed to look like the relay diagrams electricians already knew.
  • What changed: logic that required rewiring a cabinet could now be changed by editing and downloading a program.
Animation — one rung of ladder logic, energizing

This is the exact "look" the 084 borrowed from relay diagrams: two vertical power rails, contacts in between. When both contacts close, current flows and the coil on the right energizes an output.

Animation — the PLC scan cycle, running continuously

This loop is what a PLC is actually doing, forever, dozens to thousands of times per second: read every input, run the whole program against those values, write every output, then start over.

A typical scan takes well under 50 milliseconds. That speed is why a PLC can catch a fault, stop a motor, and log the event before a human would even finish seeing it happen.
09
Industry 3.0

Electronics and computing take over automation

Microprocessors, PLCs, and CNC machines together define Industry 3.0 — automation built on electronics and computing rather than purely electromechanical hardware.

Microprocessors packed the logic of an entire computer onto a single chip, making digital controllers small and affordable enough to bolt onto ordinary machine tools. CNC (Computer Numerical Control) applies that same programmable-logic idea to precision machining: a stored program of X/Y/Z coordinates drives the cutting tool, not a machinist's hand on a wheel.

10
1975 · Honeywell TDC 2000

Continuous processes needed something different

The PLC excelled at discrete, sequential control. Large continuous processes — refineries, chemical plants — needed control distributed across many areas, tied together under one operator view.

Animation — data pulsing between distributed controller nodes
  • A DCS spreads many controllers across the plant, tied together through shared communication.
  • Yokogawa's early CENTUM system appeared around the same period as the TDC 2000.
11
Supervisory control and data acquisition

One screen, many controllers

Islands of automation needed a way for a human to see and supervise all of them at once. HMI gave a visual window into a process; SCADA extended that across an entire site.

HMI (Human-Machine Interface) replaced physical gauges and indicator lamps with screens, local to one machine. SCADA (Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition) took that idea site-wide — polling many remote controllers, displaying them on one set of screens, and letting an operator issue commands back down to any of them, without leaving the control room.

1980s–1990s

One shared line, instead of a wire for everything

Point-to-point wiring for thousands of I/O points does not scale. Fieldbus let many devices share a single digital line, and report diagnostics — not just a raw value.

  • Devices report diagnostics and configuration, not just a single raw value.

2011 onward

From executing instructions to sensing and deciding

Cyber-physical systems, IIoT, edge/cloud computing, and machine learning — automation that senses, connects, analyzes, and decides.

Every dot in the animation below is a sensor, machine, or controller; every line is a live data connection. Unlike the fieldbus and Ethernet networks from the previous two sections — which mostly move a control signal from A to B — this layer exists to accumulate history: months of vibration readings, temperature trends, and cycle times, feeding models that predict a bearing failure before it happens.

  • Machine learning — historical and live data feeding predictive, not just reactive, decisions.
15
Every era, still running

Nothing got replaced. It all got layered.

Relay logic still runs simple machines. PLCs still run most discrete manufacturing. DCS still runs most continuous plants — all increasingly wrapped in IIoT and AI-assisted decisions.

Animation — every era, stacked to today
Walk any modern plant floor and you'll find all seven bars above running at once, in the same building: a relay still trips an emergency stop, a PLC still sequences a conveyor, a DCS still holds a reactor at temperature, and a cloud dashboard still predicts next month's maintenance — none of them waiting for the others to be retired first.
