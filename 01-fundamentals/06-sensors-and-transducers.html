01 · Fundamentals — Chapter 06

The physical world does not speak in PLC values.

Sensors translate physical reality into information. This chapter goes inside that translation — the physics that turns temperature, pressure, position, light, and force into an electrical signal, before any control logic ever touches it.

STAGE: PHYSICAL WORLD
Physical phenomenon → sensing element → transduction → signal conditioning → PLC live signal chain
02 — 04 · First Principles

What is a sensor, actually?

Strip away the marketing language and every sensor reduces to the same chain: a physical quantity interacts with a sensing element, produces a detectable change, and that change becomes usable information.

A sensor does not necessarily produce a PLC-compatible signal directly. A raw thermocouple outputs a few millivolts; a raw strain gauge outputs a resistance change in milliohms. Both need conditioning — which is where transducers and transmitters come in.

05 — 06 · Inside the housing

The anatomy of a sensor

Every sensor — however simple or "smart" — is built from the same four conceptual layers. Don't treat the plastic housing as a black box; open it up mentally every time.

  • Sensing element — physically touched by, or exposed to, the quantity.
  • Transduction element — converts that interaction into an electrical property.
  • Signal conditioning — amplifies, filters, linearizes.
  • Output interface — presents a usable analog, digital, or switched signal.

08 — 15 · Sensing Principles

Eight ways to turn physics into a signal

Every industrial sensor, however exotic it sounds on a datasheet, is a variation on one of these eight physical mechanisms.

01

Resistive sensors

A physical quantity changes a material's electrical resistance. The governing equation explains every variant:

R = ρL / A

Every resistive sensor disturbs one of three variables — resistivity ρ, length L, or area A:

Strain gaugeStretching a foil changes L and A
RTDTemperature changes platinum's ρ, predictably
ThermistorSemiconductor ρ changes sharply, nonlinearly
PotentiometerA wiper physically changes L in-circuit
02

Capacitive sensors

A physical quantity changes capacitance between two plates:

C = εA / d

Closing the gap d raises capacitance — the basis of proximity and displacement sensing. Changing the dielectric ε is how humidity sensors work, and how capacitive sensors detect non-metallic targets that inductive sensors can't see at all.

C ≈ 1.0×
plate distance d ↓ → capacitance C ↑ (field density rises as the gap closes)
03

Inductive sensors

OUTPUT: OFF

An oscillating coil radiates a magnetic field. A metal target entering that field induces eddy currents, which drain energy from the oscillator — its amplitude drops, a threshold trips, and an output transistor switches.

Coil → oscillating field → metal target →
eddy currents → field damped → amplitude drop →
detection circuit → output transistor → PLC

This is the most common sensor on a discrete factory floor — and it detects metal only. Plastic, wood, or a person walking past won't trigger it.

04

Magnetic sensors & the Hall effect

Current flows through a thin conductor. A perpendicular magnetic field deflects the moving charge carriers sideways, producing a measurable transverse voltage — the Hall voltage.

Hall-effect ICNon-contact, near-zero wear, used for BLDC commutation and speed sensing
Reed switchFerromagnetic contacts snap together under a field
Magnetic encoderAlternating pole ring read by a fixed Hall sensor
V_H ≈ 0 mV
05

Optical / photoelectric sensors

BEAM: CLEAR

Light travels from an emitter, interacts with an object, and reaches a photodetector. The change in light reaching the detector is the signal.

Through-beamSeparate emitter/receiver, object breaks the beam directly
Retro-reflectiveShared housing, beam bounces off a reflector
DiffuseShared housing, light bounces directly off the object
06

Thermoelectric sensors

The Seebeck effect: a temperature difference between two dissimilar metal junctions generates a small voltage — this is a thermocouple. The voltage depends on the difference between the hot and cold junction, not either temperature alone, which is why electronic cold-junction compensation is essential for an accurate reading.

07 / 08

Piezoelectric & semiconductor sensors

Piezoelectric: mechanical stress deforms a crystal lattice, generating an electrical charge on its surfaces. Excellent for vibration, impact, and dynamic force — not for static loads held over long periods, since the charge slowly leaks away through the sensor's own internal resistance.

Semiconductor: Hall ICs, temperature ICs, photodiodes, and MEMS accelerometers all combine one of the physical principles above with electronics on the same chip — the foundation that makes smart sensors possible.

Analog sensors output a continuous signal (4–20 mA, 0–10 V) proportional to the measurement.

Smart sensors add real intelligence in the housing — self-diagnosis, remote configuration, and network communication instead of a dedicated point-to-point wire.

19 — 22 · Wiring the Real World

PNP, NPN, sourcing, sinking

The most operationally important — and most commonly confused — distinction in industrial wiring. It describes how current flows through the sensor's output transistor, not simply "positive" or "negative."

PNP (sourcing) — sensor sources current TO the input

+24V
  │
Sensor
  │
  └──────→ PLC Input
NPN (sinking) — sensor sinks current FROM the input

PLC Input
   │
 Sensor
   │
  0V
PNP/NPN describes transistor output behavior; it is not simply another name for a "positive" or "negative" sensor. Mixing PNP and NPN devices on the same input card without checking compatibility is one of the most common wiring mistakes on a real panel.

23 — 27 · How Sensors Behave

Response, saturation, and drift

Real sensors don't respond in a perfectly straight line. Watch the animated sweep: the ideal response is a straight diagonal; the real response flattens near the top — it saturates. Beyond that point, more input produces no more output. A load cell pushed past its rated capacity, or a pressure transducer overranged, will silently pin at maximum instead of telling you the truth.

Drift — same input, different output over time

Day 1    →  Output A
Day 100  →  Output A + small offset
Day 500  →  Output A + larger offset

Caused by thermal aging, mechanical fatigue, electronic aging, and contamination of the sensing element — exactly why calibration programs exist.

Key characteristics, in plain terms

Sensitivity

Output change per unit of input.

Resolution

Smallest change the sensor can distinguish.

Hysteresis

Output depends on the direction the input is moving from.

Repeatability

Consistency for the same input, same conditions.

Response time

How fast the output follows a step change.

Selectivity

Responds only to the intended quantity, not others.

30 · Applied

Case studies

Conveyor object detection

An object breaks a photoelectric beam → the digital output changes state → the PLC input changes → logic executes → an actuator responds. The same animated beam sensor above is exactly this mechanism, reused at full production speed.

Motor speed measurement

A shaft-mounted encoder or Hall sensor generates pulses as the motor turns. The PLC's high-speed counter measures pulse frequency, and RPM is calculated from it.

The sensor doesn't directly "measure RPM." It generates discrete physical events — pulses — from which the receiving system calculates speed. Everything past the raw event is interpretation.

Temperature — two physical principles, same job

An RTD and a thermocouple measure the same quantity through completely different physics — resistivity change versus Seebeck voltage. RTDs are more accurate and stable but limited in range; thermocouples tolerate extreme temperatures and respond faster, at the cost of raw accuracy. Choosing between them is engineering, not habit.

33 — 34 · The Idea Worth Keeping

Sensors are not truth

A sensor provides an estimate of a physical quantity — filtered through the physics of the sensing element, the conditioning electronics, the transmission signal, and the PLC's own scaling.

Physical reality  ≠  Sensor output  ≠  PLC value  ≠  HMI display

Error and transformation are possible at every stage of that chain. An operator reading a number on an HMI is looking at the end of a long translation, not a direct window into the process.
